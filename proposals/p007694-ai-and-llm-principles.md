## Abstract

We propose project principles to guide how the Carbon project interacts with
AI-powered tools and LLMs.

## Problem

Without clear principles around how AI and LLMs specifically can be used, we are
going to have an increasing problem of vague guidelines and unclear outcomes. It
will be harder and harder for moderators and maintainers to quickly move on from
low-effort contributions and focus their time on the high-effort contributions.

## Proposal

We propose the following core principles for doing responsible and sustainable
software development in Carbon with AI-powered tools, and LLMs specifically.

First, project level principles that should guide Carbon itself as well as our
contribution principles:

-   Carbon developers are fundamentally the humans, not the tools they operate.
-   The project holds to the same high quality bar, regardless of tooling.
-   Carbon should enable developers to use tools that make them more effective.
-   AI-powered tools or LLMs should be opt-in with optionality across models or
    providers.

For the project and language as a whole, the implications of these principles is
that the Carbon project and language will invest in AI tools that enhance
productivity, provided they do not compromise code quality or create barriers
for those not using them.

We also propose a set of more precise contribution principles to govern the
development of Carbon itself:

-   Contributors are fundamentally humans.
-   Contributors provide the intent, oversight, _and accountability_ for every
    contribution, including transparency about AI tools used in it.
-   Contributors must read anything generated by LLMs that they expect others to
    read.
-   Discussions (on Discord, GitHub, etc) can use AI assistance but must have a
    full human-in-the-loop.
    -   Notably, do not paste verbatim newly generated LLM content without
        context -- if that content is useful in isolation to readers, they can
        directly query an LLM to generate it. Having a tool translate or edit
        _your_ content is fine.
-   No AI-generated media (art, images, videos, audio, etc.).
-   No requirement, expectation, or dependencies on AI tools.

And lastly, two restrictions specifically for new contributors:

-   New contributors must have sufficient expertise to understand any LLM
    generated part of their contribution.
-   New contributors should not make autonomous or fully automated
    contributions.
    -   The burden of avoiding the appearance of a fully automated contribution
        as a new contributor rests with that contributor.

Note that we do not provide a way for contributors to opt-out of encountering
AI-tooling based code or comments that satisfy these principles. We didn't find
an easy way to do this without risking fragmentation of the community. We also
hope that these principles will minimize the cost on contributors in these
circumstances. However, if contributors are having significant negative effects
due to reviewing AI-generated code or due to AI use in discussions, we encourage
them to surface that to the Carbon leads.

## Details

### Contributors are fundamentally humans

This isn't anything against automated systems, dependabot and others are great
tools. But they are still _tools_, and the fundamental intent to contribute
rests with a human. When we talk about "contributors", we're explicitly talking
about the humans in the equation.

This is especially important for an open source project, as the reason we want
new contributors is to build a community of humans. We invest in new
contributors to help build that community, much as described in the Zig blog
post about
[contributor poker](https://kristoff.it/blog/contributor-poker-and-ai/). While
we have arrived at a different policy boundary, the core idea that the community
and its contributors are humans that we are actively investing in remains.

### Contributors provide the intent, oversight, _and accountability_

The human contributor is _always_ responsible for their contribution. They own
the intent, they must ensure oversight, and they must take accountability.

That doesn't mean contributors won't make mistakes, or fail to notice something.
That is normal and already happens. The key is that they must take
responsibility for those mistakes and iteratively improve their contributions.

A key component of accountability when using AI tools is to be transparent about
their use. For example, we require the `Assisted-by:` commit message trailer
when appropriate.

### Contributors must read anything generated by LLMs that they expect other humans to read

This doesn't mean that contributors must read every single delta in every single
PR necessarily. But it means that they need to read a _proportional_ amount of
any change to what they expect others to read. This can vary across a PR or
issue post, and it must be applied to each distinct component. The PR
description is _always_ going to be read for example. But for example, a
mechanical change that a contributor verifies with tooling or spot checked is
often fine. Even then, the spot check still needs to happen, as your reviewer
would also be expected to spot check.

This requirement is key to ensuring that contributions do not become
_extractive_ -- contributors need to invest their energy and effort into
reading all parts of a contribution that they expect others in the community to
invest energy and effort into reading.

### Discussions can use AI assistance but must have a human-in-the-loop

It's fine to use AI assistance to craft messages in Carbon discussion forums,
but _every_ message must have a human-in-the-loop. Any content generated with
AI must have been reviewed _and edited_ by a human before posting it to a
Carbon forum. Content generated by AI tooling is often far more verbose and
noisy than is helpful.

Note that no one is _expected_ to use an LLM or other tool to translate or
clean up prose in discussions. We continue to ask contributors to use English,
largely to have a single consistent language in durable artifacts and ensure our
moderators don't have to understand an unbounded space of implications or
subtleties of different languages. But we're happy with imperfect English, and
will work to understand and figure out how to discuss, regardless of how much or
little automated translation tools contributors want to use.

#### Do not post verbatim content generated by an LLM in discussions

If querying an LLM is useful, readers can do that themselves. It is not
constructive to paste this content without any added context or information.

You can cite (brief) LLM output within a larger post that provides the
specific context and human commentary that you are communicating.

This only applies to content _generated by the LLM_, not your content that is
being translated, edited, or improved with the help an LLM.

### No AI-generated media (art, images, videos, audio, etc.)

Some contributors find AI-generated media fun and helpful, but others find it
distracting or actively harmful. Media creation isn't the primary goal of
Carbon, and so we take a conservative stance here to make as many contributors
able to participate in Carbon forums as possible. Only text and code should be
generated by AI tools as part of any contribution.

### No requirement, expectation, or dependencies on AI tools

We are actively interested in continuing to support and engage with contributors
and communities that do _not_ want to use AI tooling. As a consequence, we
always want AI tooling to be optional for contributors. We don't want to rely on
AI tooling as the _only_ way to solve a given problem, as that would leave
contributors who object to these tools unable to make progress.

### New contributors must have sufficient expertise to understand any LLM generated part of their contribution

This doesn't mean new contributors must be certain their contribution is
_correct_, and there can always be parts of the codebase or system that they
are not yet familiar with. But they need to have the basic expertise to
understand the _change being made_ and why that change matches their intent.

This also doesn't mean that new contributors cannot work on efforts that they
don't have the expertise to understand at the start. The requirement is that if
and when new contributors do that, they need to both develop their own
expertise _and_ the contribution. New contributors can join Discord or
discussions, ask questions, and work to expand their expertise. But if a new
contributor doesn't understand what the LLM is generating, they should not
contribute it. This largely means starting small and with things that don't
require deep knowledge of the toolchain internals, and building up over time

### New contributors should not make autonomous or fully automated contributions

This is specifically guidance for new contributors, for example those without
commit access. Contributors with commit access that would like to set up
automation should discuss that and gain consensus in the community. The
community has sufficient access and resources to operate any amount of
automation that would be beneficial. New contributors are not contributing
anything meaningful by setting up and operating automation themselves,
especially without coordinating with the community. At best, it duplicates
existing infrastructure. More often, there are reasons why the project doesn't
need that specific kind of automation, and so these contributions have no value
to the project and community. They also don't help new contributors develop
expertise in the project and expand the scope of their contributions.

This also applies to systematic trivial fixes like fixing typos, misspellings,
or punctuation. Doing this systematically across the codebase is not acceptable
from new contributors. If in doubt, ask maintainers before making any kind of
systematic change.

#### New contributors bear the burden of avoiding the appearance of a fully automated contribution

The trust afforded to a contributor is a function of their track record of
contributions. As a consequence, the project doesn't spend time trying to
determine if a contribution is truly autonomous or not. New contributors must
inherently build expertise in order to make non-trivial contributions according
to this policy. If that hasn't happened, we will close the PR and suggest
discussing the change with the community if they want to pursue it. That
discussion can establish expertise even for a new contributor that unblocks
making the change, and we encourage new contributors to start by discussing
things with the community.

## Rationale

-   [Community and culture](/docs/project/goals.md#community-and-culture)
    -   This works to strike a balance between our community and culture being
        open to both developers and contributors who _do_ use AI tooling and
        those who do not.
    -   It also works to address the scaling challenges our community would face
        from unbounded AI-powered contributions.
-   [Language tools and ecosystem](/docs/project/goals.md#language-tools-and-ecosystem)
    -   The stance of enabling users who _do_ use AI tooling to do so
        effectively aligns with Carbon's general goal of being a "batteries
        included" project and aggressively enabling tool usage throughout the
        project and for its users. For at least some of its users, that includes
        AI tooling.
    -   Similarly, enabling users who _do not_ use AI tooling to still use
        Carbon effectively and to retain broad access to expected Carbon tooling
        aligns with being "batteries included" for those users.

## Alternatives considered

### Completely ban generative AI and LLMs in contributions

Many open source projects have chosen a simpler strategy: simply banning all AI
contributions.

Advantages:

-   Simple and unambiguous policy
-   Inherently provides a basis for protecting the project from harmful AI
    contributions
-   Avoids any potential objections to Carbon due to the use of AI contributions

Disadvantages:

-   We have seen specific cases where, when used carefully, the project can
    significantly benefit from AI-powered contributions.
-   Developers with extensive experience using AI tools would be unable to
    contribute using their preferred and most productive set of tools.
-   We would have to remove significant functionality in Carbon that has been
    developed heavily with the help of AI tools to fully capture the benefit.

Currently, the project is successfully capturing significant value from use of
these tools within the limits these principles outline, and we want to continue
to get those benefits.

While several open source projects, notably [Zig] and [Rust], are sharply
restricting AI tool usage or completely banning it, other open source projects
are taking a different stance. For example, [LLVM], [Django], and [Ghostty],
have all adopted similar guidance to what we are proposing, and are working to
enable _some_ AI powered contributions in ways that are constructive for the
community. LLVM's stance is of special interest as in general we work hard to
align Carbon policies with LLVM policies so that the communities can collaborate
and share as freely as possible.

[Zig]: https://ziglang.org/code-of-conduct/#strict-no-llm-no-ai-policy
[Rust]: https://blog.rust-lang.org/inside-rust/2026/08/05/rust-langrust-is-adopting-an-llm-policy/
[LLVM]: https://llvm.org/docs/AIToolPolicy.html
[Django]: https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/dev/internals/contributing/writing-code/submitting-patches/#ai-assisted-contributions
[Ghostty]: https://github.com/ghostty-org/ghostty/blob/main/AI_POLICY.md

Debian also recently held a community vote that landed on an
[option](https://www.debian.org/vote/2026/vote_002#texte) that is very similar
to the stance being taken by Carbon. While Debian is a Linux distribution and
very different from Carbon in this regard, it has consistently been a leader in
the broader open source community, and represents one of the larger community
_votes_ on specific policies here. We see the alignment between the outcome of
this vote and what is proposed as a sign that this is an effective compromise
for an open source project.

### Yolo-mode / no restrictions on AI usage

The other simple option is to have no restrictions on AI usage in the project.

However, there are a number of drawbacks that motivated this policy:

-   Unbounded AI contributions have consistently proven to be non-viable for
    open source projects due to the influx of low-effort and low-value
    contributions.
-   Significantly exacerbates the problem of sustaining our high quality bar in
    the face of widespread AI contributions.
-   Alienates a large number of developers who do not want to interact with
    these tools at all, or with usage of the tools that doesn't conform to our
    policy.
